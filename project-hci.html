Usability testing

Abstract

On Tuesday, October 21st, 2025, group members David, Eli, Favour, Tyrone, and Divine conducted a usability test on a Japanese travel website Hakone Japan, to test the functionality and usability of the website. The purpose of this report is to provide our observations, collected data, analysis, and interpretation of our findings, and use this data to improve design decisions that directly affect users. We tested 10 participants in the adjunct faculty offices on the second floor of the York Building, from 12:30 pm to 1:45pm. Our process included each member of the group testing the website, creating questions to ask the participant before and after the test, compiling tasks to give the participants, generating a consent form, discussing key metrics to gather for our data collection, assigning roles to each member to ensure smooth operations during test day, multiple dry runs the day before to prepare for the test, and finally, carrying out the test. By the end of our report, we hope to have demonstrated our understanding of conducting a usability test through our data accuracy, mitigation of biases, and methodologies, and highlight how invaluable gathering real data from real users can be for improving user experience and guiding evidence-based design decisions.

Description of Website Evaluated

The Hakone Japan website, developed by the Hakone Tourist Association, is designed as a one-stop shop for tourists looking to plan a visit to Hakone. The home page features a slideshow showcasing some of Hakone’s landscapes, attractions, and cultural events, with the text “Hakone: Experience Wonder” displayed in the middle of the slideshow. Tabs “Home”, “Home”, “Discover”, “Things to Do”, and “Plan Your Trip” are located in the top right-hand section of the website, along with a search bar icon located in the top right corner. The image below is what users are first presented with when visiting the website.

Moving down the home page, users are presented with the title: “A place to experience and connect with nature and history”, some text below the title, and block sections that correspond to different areas of the website. These block sections 2 and 3 describe tourist information such as “Hakone Digital Map/Traffic Information”, “Summer in Hakone 2025”, “Hakone Sustainable Tourism”, “Hakone Local Tour”, and “Hakone Live Views”.



As we continue scrolling down the home page, we come across other relevant sections such as Discover Hakone, Things to Do,Plan Your Trip, and Hakone Travel Ideas. These subsections host important information for tourists about where to find activities, transportation, experiences, and places to stay

Besides the home page, there are many other pages and links that users can visit when interacting with the website. However, the home page is the most important, as it’s the first page users are presented with.

Logistics and Methodology of the Usability Testing

Summary

The study was conducted on October 21st, 2025. Each study was considered started when the participant handed in both their consent and pre-questionnaire form. We began the study with the first participant at 12:26 PM and finished with the last participant at 1:54 PM. The study was conducted in room 2.. on the 2nd floor of 7800 York Rd. The purpose of our study was to test the usability and functionality of the Hakone, Japan tourist website. At the beginning of each test, we first greeted the participant and thanked them for joining our usability test. We then briefed the participant on the procedure of the study and what was to take place during the study. We reassured each participant that they are not being tested and that they are free to leave at any moment without penalty. Then we had the participant fill out a consent form and answer a short pre-questionnaire form. Once completed, the data completion team stepped in and took over. The data collection team conducted the study with each participant while noting observations and recording performance metrics. Upon completion of the study, the data collection team took the participant to David who was waiting in the hallway, to complete the post-questionnaire.

      The study/data collection process was as follows:

      Once our introduction team, which consisted of Eli and Tyrone, finished, our data collection team stepped in and took over. The members of our data collection team were Divine and Favour. Once in the room, we first greeted the participant and then proceeded with instructions. We told the participant that they would be completing a series of tasks on the website. We instructed them, saying, “we will let you know when to begin each task. Once you have completed the task, write down your answer in the designated area on the task list and let us know that you are finished. We will then instruct you to move on to the next task."

      Divine then walked over and opened up the website. We instructed the participant to first find a place to stay. Once finished with that task and recorded their answer on the form provided, we then instructed them to find a restaurant or food that they like. And once they finish with that task, we then instructed them to find an activity to do while they are there. During each task, Favour monitored the time it took them to complete the task, by starting a stopwatch when they started and stopping it when they completed the task. While Favour monitored the time on task, Divine monitored the number of pages opened in order to complete the task. These were the two metrics that we measured. Once all the tasks were completed, Divine walked over and cleared the cache and closed the browser window before opening the door to prevent people standing outside from seeing the website. We then thanked the participant for participating in our study and told them that they will be going with one of our other group members to complete a short post-questionnaire. We then took the participant outside the room to meet David, who was waiting in the hallway to take them and complete the post questionnaire. After handing the participant off to David, we went back in the room and prepared it for the next participant. We took the participant’s form and put it in our stack of completed forms, which were lying face down on the table. We then took a new consent and pre-questionnaire form and put it at the test station for the next participant. This concluded the study/data collection process for each participant.

      Results and Evaluation(Includes our Briefing Notes)



    On average, participants opened 3.9 pages for Task 1, 3.4 pages for Task 2, and 3.1 pages for Task 3. The average completion times were 1 minute 17 seconds for Task 1, 1 minute for Task 2, and 42 seconds for Task 3. Limitations in our study are being unable to pick where the study takes place, the amount time per participant, and the demographic.

    Data collection Observations

    While every participant did complete all the tasks, there were some noticeable differences in the participants’ strategies for completing tasks. There were also some habits observed from the participants during the study.

    • For example, user 2 kept on clicking the mouse multiple times because the site wasn’t loading fast. It wasn’t a deadlink, the site just took some time to load the page and the user either kept clicking the link multiple times or went to a different page.
    • User 3 opened a whole different site, outside of the main site that the study was being conducted on. He clicked on a link that took him out of the site, and completed the task that way. This user also utilized the search bar when looking for food, which most people did not do.
    • The 4th participant didn’t utilize the navigation menu at all. He simply scrolled on the home page till he found what he was looking for or found a link that took him to that section.
    • Participant 5 looked through multiple sections when trying to find a restaurant/food. They initially used the navigation menu, but then just started scrolling through the pages instead.
    • User 6 went out of order. We asked him to find a place to stay as the first task and instead he found a restaurant/food.

    As noted by the list above, all participants did complete the tasks but there were differences in how they achieved it.

    Usability Problems Noted

    As a Data Collector, it was our job to keep note of 100 percent everything. The most common problem we noted was clicking outside links, and users skipping a task. For instance, we asked a user to identify a place to stay in Hakone, Japan, and they gave us an answer of ‘Things to do’. Problems like this messed up the time slot for the exact question asked. We took note of issues like this. The website had a few tabs that did not have a picture to show the users what it exactly looked like. For instance, when the website was displaying things to do inside of Hakone, Japan, some did not have a visual representation of what it looked like, which somewhat tempted users to look up what it represents on Google images.

    Crucial information like “How to Get to Hakone’ was under broad categories instead of being immediately visible on the homepage.

    Users scrolled past useful tabs, which was due to smaller fonts on a few essential tabs.

    Found a few dead links which were very important for booking transportation in Hakone, Japan.

    What Did You Learn?

    DATA COLLECTION TEAM

    Favour and Divine were in charge of the Data collection team. We were able to obtain quantifiable statistics that validated user input by recording time and clicks. We discovered how qualitative observations (like hesitation or perplexity) can be supported by quantitative measurements (like time and clicks).

    As Data collectors, we had to stay attentive to every click and movement; every second counted, and every click was crucial. It was our job to make sure things ran smoothly.

    We learned that users navigate interfaces in different ways; we had users who completed the task in seconds, and we had some who took a long time.

    Some users did not follow basic instructions, like answering a question that was not asked or clicking out of the website; others were able to utilize the search feature and saved time to complete their tasks. We had a few candidates who asked us a question that may give a hint at the answer; our job was to make sure the usability test was unbiased and ethical.

    INTRODUCTION & PRE-QUESTIONNAIRE TEAM

    Our team learned a lot during this usability test. One of the first things that stood out was that, regardless of how structured each section of the study was, you couldn’t predict how participants would act. Our team had a consistent flow of operations. When going to retrieve the participant, we walked on the right side of the hallway to the conference room. We always announced, “We are ready for our next participant”. Once the participant walked to the door, we always brought the participant back the same way we arrived. When inside the room, we read our introduction, asked them if they had any questions, gave them the pre-questionnaire and consent form, and let them know a team member would be coming into the room shortly to assist them.

    However, even as structured as that process was, it was surprising to see how each participant reacted. One participant was already standing at the doorway when we arrived at the conference room to collect the next person. Some participants fidgeted with the computer screen before stopping and listening to our directions. Some participants engaged in conversation with the team as we were bringing them back to the room.

    Another minor detail that we didn’t expect was that we would have a sign-in sheet. We had to decide whether we wanted to ask the participants after participant 1 to sign in to the sign-in sheet or not, because we wanted to stay consistent. We decided to let the participant sign in if they noticed the sheet and if not, after administering the consent and pre-questionnaire form, we would explicitly tell them.

    This experience taught us to be flexible and read the room. We have no idea if the participants were experiencing a good or bad day, or were stressed from school or home, or were alert or sleepy. We, as researchers, cannot predict those facts. What’s important is that we report the news, not create it. Authentic and genuine data produce invaluable insights that can change the way a product is received.

    POST-QUESTIONAIRE Team

    The post-survey questions provided valuable insights that the numerical data from the tasks alone couldn't capture. By asking participants to rate their experience and elaborate immediately after the tasks, I saw clear patterns.

    I observed contradictions where a participant responded “N/A” for Question 1 (“If you were asked to improve the website for new travelers, what features or information would you add?”), or would rate the site's inspiration to visit that country, but followed up with a "No" when asked if they could rely on the site entirely. This taught us that participants associate "completable" with "easy," emphasizing the need to use both a rating scale and an open-ended question to get a true gauge of usability. Many participants also used Question 1 as a moment to vent their primary frustration and write a deeper explanation.

    Our process of walking the participant to the next room, and then giving them the post-questions, showed that participant attention slightly drops after the main task is finished. Most participants took a moment to recall what happened in order or answer the questions.
